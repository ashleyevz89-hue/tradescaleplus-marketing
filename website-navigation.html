The problem

Buyers give up in the filters.

You pay for every visitor on your website, through your stock advertising, your Google spend, your social. And then you hand them a filter panel and hope. On a big forecourt, that is where deals quietly die: the buyer who wanted a specific thing scrolls three pages, cannot find it, and leaves for a site that made it easier. The car they wanted may well have been in your stock the whole time.

The assistant removes that whole step. Instead of making buyers search like a database administrator, it lets them talk like a customer.

Questions, answered

Car finding, answered.

How does the assistant find the right car?

The buyer describes what they want in plain English, body type, fuel, gearbox, colour, budget, even specific features, and the assistant matches it against your live stock in seconds, then takes them straight to the matching car's page on your own website.

What if the buyer's budget doesn't quite fit?

It matches on budget with a sensible stretch band, so a buyer with £12,000 to spend also sees the right car at £12,400 rather than nothing at all.

Does it ever show sold or reserved cars?

No, never. It only offers cars that are actually available, because it relearns your stock every day.

Does it replace my website's search filters?

It sits alongside them. Filters work for buyers who know exactly what they want; the assistant works for everyone else, the buyers who would otherwise give up and leave.

Does this work on a big superstore site?

That is exactly where it earns the most. The bigger the forecourt, the harder it is to browse and the more buyers the filters lose. See our page for used-car superstores.
